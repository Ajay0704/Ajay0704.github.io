The Challenge

Every SaaS company in 2026 is asking the same two questions: is per-seat pricing dying, and how should we charge for AI? The industry answers with confident statistics — for example, an ICONIQ Capital survey of roughly 300 AI-building software companies, published by BCG, found 68% of vendors charge separately for AI or restrict it to premium tiers.

The trouble is the credible estimates measure different things and don’t agree — Kyle Poyar’s 230-company survey finds 29% use AI credits, a narrower measure, while the ICONIQ/BCG figure is 68% — and the highest numbers come from aggregator pages with no stated method. There was no open dataset to settle it.

So I built one: I collected pricing data from 198 leading SaaS companies directly from their live pricing pages, and used it to answer the questions with evidence instead of citation.

What I Did

Sampling. 198 recognisable B2B SaaS companies, 18 in each of 11 categories — dev tools, data & analytics, CRM, martech, collaboration, design, security, HR, fintech-ops, support and AI-native — equal numbers per category, so no single segment dominates the totals.

Collection. Each live pricing page was read and converted into 19 structured fields (23 columns in the public dataset): pricing model, tier names, entry price, how AI is charged, where SSO sits. Prices normalised to USD per month. No third-party datasets, no synthetic data.

Recovery. The most valuable pages fight back — Salesforce, OpenAI, Adobe and Canva block scrapers or render prices in JavaScript. Those were recovered with a headless browser, and 14 hard-blocked pages were collected by hand, lifting coverage from 80% to 95%.

Validation. Every headline count was recomputed from the published dataset and matches the analysis pipeline exactly. That confirms the arithmetic, not the accuracy of each coded field, so a separate accuracy audit of a random sample of rows is under way. Every row in the working dataset stores its source URL and a quoted snippet of the page text, so any value can be checked. In October 2026 an audit re-checked every disputed row against archived copies of the August pages; the 111 resulting field corrections are logged with their sources.

Where the Published Numbers Break Down

The headline finding is that the most-cited AI-pricing statistic is far higher than what public pricing pages show. I am not claiming the reports are dishonest — I am claiming three specific things: credible sources disagree (a 230-company survey says 29% use AI credits, ICONIQ/BCG says 68% charge separately or premium-only), the higher figures are not reproducible from public pricing pages, and the gap comes from different definitions, populations and methods. Self-reported “we monetise AI” is a much easier bar than “the pricing page actually charges you more.”

42% vs 68%

Like for like, public pricing pages show fewer companies using AI credits (21%) than Poyar’s survey reports (29%), and fewer charging extra in any form (42%) than ICONIQ reports (68%). Both surveys are self-reported and sample different companies. Part of the gap is likely AI sold in enterprise contracts that never appears on a public pricing page, so this measures visible pricing only.

Key Outcomes

  • Self-collected a dataset of 188 SaaS companies × 26 columns, every value traced to a pricing page — 95% coverage of the sampling frame.
  • Found that 42% of AI-offering companies visibly charge extra for AI on their pricing pages, well below the 68% reported in self-reported surveys.
  • Quantified the “SSO tax” (69% of pages that list SSO gate it to the top tier) and built benchmarks that did not previously exist publicly (median 4 tiers; $14 median entry price; 20% median annual discount among the 85 companies that state one).
  • Built an interactive dashboard so anyone can filter the data by category and check the findings themselves.
  • Documented limitations openly, including sample bias and small category sample sizes.
  • Published the full dataset for anyone to check: download the 188-company CSV ↓
