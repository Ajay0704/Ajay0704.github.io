The State of SaaS Pricing 2026

Why this project exists, what the data says, and whether the widely-cited industry numbers hold up.

191 companies analysed (of 198 attempted · 96%) · 11 categories · snapshot August 2026 · Ajay Karthick

1. Why this project exists

In 2026, two questions dominate SaaS pricing: is per-seat pricing dying, and how should companies charge for AI? The industry answers with confident statistics. The most-repeated one is that 73% of vendors now charge separately for AI features.

The problem is that nobody can check it. That figure — and every figure like it — comes from vendor surveys and aggregator reports whose samples aren't published and whose definitions aren't stated. And when you line up the published estimates for the same question, they don't agree with each other:

SourceClaimFigure
McKinseyMonetised AI as a standalone product16%
High Alpha / ZyloFormally monetising AI features41%
BCGRestricted AI to premium tiers68%
CloudNuroCharge separately for AI73%

That is a 4.5× spread on the same underlying question. They cannot all be right, and there was no public dataset to settle it.

So the project's purpose was not to produce charts. It was to build the missing evidence: go to 200 live pricing pages, record what they actually say, and publish a dataset anyone can open and argue with.

2. What we did

  • Sample: 198 recognisable B2B SaaS companies, 18 in each of 11 categories, so no segment dominates the result.
  • Collection: each company's live pricing page was read and converted into 23 structured fields — pricing model, tiers, entry price, how AI is charged, where SSO sits. Prices normalised to USD per month.
  • Recovery: pages that block scrapers or render prices in JavaScript (Salesforce, OpenAI, Adobe, Canva) were recovered with a real browser and manual verification, lifting coverage from 80% to 96%.
  • Result: 191 usable companies. The 7 unresolved are explained, not hidden: 3 were acquired mid-study (Coda, Airbase, Drift); 4 publish no public price at all (Workday, Namely, Iterable, Paylocity).

Per-seat is still the largest single model, but a majority of companies now price some other way. The detail that matters: the second-place model is hybrid, not pure usage. The market is not swapping seats for meters — it is layering usage charges on top of a seat-based base. More companies hide their price entirely (25) than run a pure meter (23).

4. Are the other reports wrong?

This needs a precise answer, not a slogan.

We are not claiming the published reports are lying. We are claiming three specific things:

  • They disagree with each other by 4.5× (16% to 73%) on the same question. So “73%” is not settled fact — it is one estimate among several that contradict one another.
  • The 73% figure is not reproducible from public pricing pages. We checked 191 of them. If nearly three-quarters of vendors charged separately for AI, it would be visible on those pages. It is not.
  • The gap is largely definitional. “Charges separately for AI” can mean “has any paid AI option anywhere in the company’s catalogue” — which is easy to satisfy. We measured something stricter and more useful to a buyer: “the pricing page shows you paying more because of AI.” Both numbers can be true; they answer different questions.
Supporting evidence that our number is the credible one: our measurement of 34% sits between McKinsey’s 16% and Zylo’s 41% — the two independent research estimates. The outliers are the aggregator figures (68%, 73%). We land inside the cluster of rigorous sources, not outside it.

5. Our answer

34%

On a checkable sample of 191 leading SaaS companies, roughly one in three charges extra for AI — not the widely-cited three in four. The most common decision in the market today is to bundle AI into the existing subscription and compete on it, rather than sell it as a separate line item.

And on the other headline question: per-seat pricing is not dying — it is becoming the base layer of a hybrid model. Meanwhile 69% of vendors gate basic security behind their most expensive plan.

What this is useful for. A product manager deciding whether to charge for a new AI feature now has a market answer: most competitors don't, and charging makes you the outlier. A founder setting pricing has benchmarks (4 tiers, 20% annual discount, $10 median entry). A buyer negotiating an Enterprise quote can see that the SSO upcharge is standard practice across 69% of vendors, not a special case. An investor can check whether a company's pricing is normal for its category. None of that was answerable from public sources before.

6. What we cannot claim

  • This describes leading SaaS, not all SaaS. The 198 companies were chosen for recognisability, not at random. Results describe the top of the market, not the median startup.
  • It is a single-day snapshot (August 2026). Pricing pages change constantly.
  • Category-level breakdowns are directional only. Some category bars rest on very few companies — the CRM SSO figure is 6 of 7 companies, HR is 2 of 8. Headline numbers use the full sample and are solid; category splits should not be read as precise.
  • Extraction is roughly 90% accurate at field level. Every row carries its source URL and a quoted snippet of the text it was read from, so any individual value can be audited.
  • We cannot prove intent. We can show that 69% gate SSO behind the top tier. We cannot prove from a pricing page why they do it.
