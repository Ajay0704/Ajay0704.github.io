The State of SaaS Pricing 2026

Why this project exists, what the data says, and whether the widely-cited industry numbers hold up.

188 companies analysed (of 198 attempted · 95%) · 11 categories · snapshot August 2026 · Ajay Karthick

1. Why this project exists

In 2026, two questions dominate SaaS pricing: is per-seat pricing dying, and how should companies charge for AI? The industry answers with confident statistics. A widely-cited one, from an ICONIQ Capital survey of roughly 300 AI-building software companies, published by BCG, is that 68% of vendors charge separately for AI or restrict it to their premium tiers.

The trouble is these estimates measure different things and don't agree — and the highest, most-repeated figures come from aggregator pages with no stated method. Line up the two sources that do name a method:

SourceWhat it measuresFigure
Kyle Poyar, Growth Unhinged (self-reported survey, n=230)Use AI credits29%
ICONIQ Capital (via BCG, 2025; self-reported, AI-building companies)Charge separately for AI, or restrict it to premium tiers68%

That is more than a 2× gap between two named sources, driven by definition and by who was asked — one counts AI credits only, the other also counts “AI is only in the premium tier.” And unattributed aggregator pages push the figure as high as 73% with no stated method at all. No single number is settled, and there was no public dataset to check them against.

So the project's purpose was not to produce charts. It was to build the missing evidence: go to 200 live pricing pages, record what they actually say, and publish a dataset anyone can open and argue with.

2. What we did

  • Sample: 198 recognisable B2B SaaS companies, 18 in each of 11 categories, so no segment dominates the result.
  • Collection: each company's live pricing page was read and converted into 19 structured fields (23 columns in the public dataset) — pricing model, tiers, entry price, how AI is charged, where SSO sits. Prices normalised to USD per month.
  • Recovery: pages that block scrapers or render prices in JavaScript (Salesforce, OpenAI, Adobe, Canva) were recovered with a real browser, and 14 hard-blocked pages were collected by hand, lifting coverage from 80% to 95%.
  • Result: 188 usable companies. The 10 unresolved are explained, not hidden: 3 were acquired mid-study (Coda, Airbase, Drift); 4 pricing pages returned errors or no pricing content (Workday, Namely, Iterable, Paylocity); 3 never rendered their prices, even in archived copies (Evernote, ActiveCampaign, Dashlane).
  • Audit: in October 2026 every disputed row was re-checked against the Wayback Machine copy of the page nearest 18 August 2026, or against the saved quote. 111 field corrections across 78 companies are logged in data/corrections.csv with the value replaced, the reason and the source.

Per-seat is still the largest single model, but a majority of companies now price some other way. The detail that matters: the second-place model is hybrid, not pure usage. The market is not swapping seats for meters — it is layering usage charges on top of a subscription. Counting hybrids that also bill per seat, about 6 in 10 companies whose page shows how they charge (101 of 169) still charge per seat somewhere.

Finding 3 — The “SSO tax” is standard practice

Single sign-on is a basic security feature. Of the 120 companies whose pricing page mentions it, 83 (69%) require their top paid tier or an Enterprise contract to get it.

Finding 4 — Benchmarks that didn't previously exist

Median company offers 4 tiers and starts paid plans at a median of $14 per month (across the 151 companies with a listed entry price; $11 for per-seat plans). Among the 85 companies that state an annual discount, the median is 20%. 85% publish a real price, and 85% still keep a “contact sales” tier above it.

4. Are the other reports wrong?

This needs a precise answer, not a slogan.

We are not claiming the published reports are lying. We are claiming three specific things:

  • Credible sources disagree, and measure different things. A named 230-company survey (Growth Unhinged) finds 29% use AI credits; an ICONIQ survey via BCG finds 68% charge separately or keep AI to premium tiers; unattributed aggregator pages cite 73%. So no single figure is settled fact.
  • Public pricing pages show a lower rate. We checked 188 of them. Some AI pricing, especially in enterprise contracts, never appears on a public page, so this study measures visible pricing only.
  • The gap comes from definitions, populations and method. The surveys asked executives at the companies they sampled; we read the public pricing pages of well-known SaaS companies and recorded whether “the pricing page shows you paying more because of AI.” Both numbers can be true; they answer different questions.
Like for like: on the same measure as Poyar’s survey, public pricing pages show 21% of AI-offering companies using AI credits, versus his 29%. On ICONIQ’s broader measure, 42% charge extra in any form, versus 68%. Our figures come from a different method and a different set of companies, so the comparison shows how far visible pricing sits from self-reported pricing, not that either survey is wrong.

5. Our answer

42%

On a checkable sample of 188 leading SaaS companies, 42% of those that offer AI visibly charge extra for it on their pricing page — well below the 68% reported in surveys. The most common single decision is still to bundle AI into the existing subscription, rather than sell it as a separate line item.

And on the other headline question: per-seat is still the largest single model (46%) but not a majority; second is a subscription-plus-usage hybrid (22%). Among pages that list SSO, 69% gate it to their most expensive plan.

What this is useful for. A product manager deciding whether to charge for a new AI feature now has a market answer: most competitors don't visibly charge extra for it. A founder setting pricing has benchmarks (4 tiers, $14 median entry price, 20% median annual discount among companies that state one). A buyer negotiating an Enterprise quote can see that the SSO upcharge is standard practice across 69% of vendors that list SSO, not a special case. An investor can check whether a company's pricing is normal for its category. None of that was answerable from public sources before.

6. What we cannot claim

  • This describes leading SaaS, not all SaaS. The 198 companies were chosen for recognisability, not at random. Results describe the top of the market, not the median startup.
  • It is a single-day snapshot (August 2026). Pricing pages change constantly.
  • Category-level breakdowns are directional only. Some category bars rest on very few companies — the CRM SSO figure is 6 of 7 companies, HR is 2 of 7. Headline numbers use the full sample; category splits should not be read as precise.
  • Field-level accuracy is being measured. A separate accuracy audit of a random sample of rows is under way. Every row in the working dataset stores its source URL and a quoted snippet of the text it was read from, so any individual value can be checked.
  • We cannot prove intent. We can show that 69% of pages listing SSO gate it behind the top tier. We cannot prove from a pricing page why they do it.
